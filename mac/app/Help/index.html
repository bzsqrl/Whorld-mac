Whorld for Mac

A native macOS port of Whorld, the free sacred-geometry visualizer by Chris Korda.

Whorld draws animated rings of polygons and stars. You change how they grow, spin, skew and color with the parameters, and play them live with the keyboard or a MIDI controller. The Mac version is based on Whorld V2 and draws a patch the same way the Windows version does.

The detailed documentation for each parameter and feature is the Whorld manual. It was written for Whorld V1 on Windows, so some of what it says about menus, dialogs and installation differs from this app; this page lists the differences.

Files

Patches (.whp), playlists (.whl), snapshots (.whs) and movies (.whm) are the same files Windows Whorld uses, and can be moved between the two in either direction. Double-click a patch or playlist in the Finder to open it, or choose it from File > Open Recent. When you quit, Whorld remembers the playlist and patch you had open (the patch file, or the playlist entry that was playing) and opens them again next time; an untitled patch or a playlist that was never saved starts fresh, and a file that has since been moved is still found. To start with an untitled patch instead, as Windows Whorld does, turn off Whorld > Settings > General > Reopen the last playlist and patch at launch. Playing a patch from the playlist (or by MIDI or OSC) replaces the one showing without asking to save it, so you can move between patches freely while performing; to keep changes, save them first (⌘S). File > New, File > Open and Quit still ask. A patch can name its author: the window's title bar and the top of the Properties panel show the playing patch's name and author, and the Playlist has an Author column. Set the author in the Save As dialog's Author field, or with File > Patch Author… (then save). For a patch that has no author yet, both suggest Whorld > Settings > General > Your name. The author is a line of its own in the patch file, which Windows Whorld ignores (and leaves out if it saves the patch again).

Windows and panels

As on Windows, the Whorld window holds the picture in the middle and the panels around it: Parameters, Master, Globals, Properties, Playlist and MIDI Mapping. Show or hide each from the View menu (shortcuts below). The panels sit in a left, a right and a bottom area; drag the dividers between them to resize. To move a panel, drag its title bar onto another panel: near one of that panel's edges, it goes above, below, left or right of it; on its title bar or in its middle, the two are stacked as tabs, as on Windows (what will happen is highlighted while you drag). Click a tab to show its panel, and drag a tab to move that panel out of the stack. A panel's View menu shortcut brings it to the front when it's a tab behind another. Or drop it near the left, right or bottom edge of the picture to add it to that area, or use the ⋯ menu on its title bar, which can also float it in a window of its own (its Dock button puts it back). Window > Show All Panels shows every panel and fits the Whorld window to its screen; Window > Reset Layout puts everything back in its standard place: Parameters on the left beside the picture, and Playlist, Properties, Master and Globals along the bottom. The arrangement is remembered between launches. To keep an arrangement for later, choose Window > Save Layout…, which saves the panels, their sizes and the windows in a .whlayout file; Window > Open Layout… (or double-clicking the file) brings it back, adapted to the screen. A playlist can carry a layout too: tick Include the panel layout in the Save Playlist As dialog, and opening the playlist arranges the panels that way (turn this off in Whorld > Settings > General > Open playlists with their layout). Windows Whorld ignores a playlist's layout. In the panels, click a slider and use ← → to move it in fine steps, ⇧← ⇧→ for larger steps, and ⇧⌘← ⇧⌘→ to jump to either end; in a number box, ↑ ↓ do the same (fine, with ⇧ larger, with ⇧⌘ to the end of the range, where the range has one). Hover over a control's name in Parameters, Master, Globals or Properties for a short explanation of what it does (Whorld > Settings > General > Show tips on control names turns these off). Right-click (or Control-click) a slider or number box to set it back to its value in the patch as it was loaded (opened, played from the playlist, or new) or last saved. On a trackpad, rest the pointer on a slider and swipe sideways with two fingers to move it, or on a number box and swipe up or down to step it (with ⇧ held, in larger steps). A mouse wheel works too: over a slider each click moves it 1% of its range, and over a number box each click is one step (larger with ⇧). A swipe or turn of the wheel that starts anywhere else scrolls the panel as usual, even as controls pass under the pointer; pause for half a second before turning the wheel over a control that scrolling brought under the pointer. In Properties, click a row, then ↑ ↓ move through the list and Space toggles the row (elsewhere, Space pauses). The options dialog is Whorld > Settings (⌘,).

Window > Detach (⇧⌘D) moves the picture into a window of its own, as on Windows, and the panels then fill the Whorld window. Detach again, or close the picture's window, to put it back.

Full screen and projectors

View > Enter Full Screen (⇧⌘F) fills the display the Whorld window is on; ⇧⌘F or Esc returns to the window. F11, the Windows shortcut, works too where the keyboard sends it to apps; on most Mac keyboards F11 is a media key, and fn-F11 shows the desktop. To show Whorld on a projector or second display, choose Window > Detach, drag the picture's window onto that display, then press ⇧⌘F. On a second display the menu bar and Dock stay on your main display, so the panels remain usable there while the picture fills the projector. On the main display, full screen hides the menu bar and Dock (they don't slide in; press ⇧⌘F or Esc to leave). If the second display is disconnected, Whorld leaves full screen. In full screen the pointer is hidden while it's over the picture. In full screen, or with the pointer over the picture, ⌘↓ plays the playlist's next patch and ⌘↑ the previous one (after the last comes the first again; with none playing, they start at the first or the last), and with snapshots loaded, ⌘→ and ⌘← show the next and previous snapshot. Over the picture, in the window or full screen, scrolling (a two-finger swipe up or down, or the mouse wheel) zooms, and with ⇧ changes Damping. Hold a key while you scroll to change something else instead (up increases); with the key still held, a two-finger tap (or a right click) puts that control back the same way, to its value when the patch was loaded or last saved. Window > Key Map… lists which key moves which control, and lets you change them. To change a key, click it and press the new one; a key can move one control, and assigning a key that's in use takes it from the other row. Any parameter column (Value, Amplitude, Frequency, Pulse Width, Global), Master row, Hue or the origin can have a key, each with its own speed. The starting map puts ring shape on the top letter row (Q Ring Spacing, W Line Width, E Ring Growth, R Rings, T Ring Sides, Y Star Factor, U Pinwheel, I Rotate Speed, O Aspect Ratio, P Skew Radius, [ Skew Angle), color on the home row (A Color Speed, S Speed, D Damping, F Fade, G Saturation, H Hue, J Lightness, K Alpha, L Hue Span), Master and the background on the bottom row (Z Zoom, X Trail, C Canvas, V Copies, B Tempo, N Spread, M Bk Hue, comma Bk Lightness, period Bk Saturation, slash Bk Alpha), and curves and the origin on the digits (1 Even Curve, 2 Odd Curve, 3 Even Shear, 4 Odd Shear, 9 Origin X, 0 Origin Y); Restore Defaults brings it back, and Export and Import keep a map in a file. The map is a setting, the same for every patch and playlist. How far each swipe goes is Settings > Control > Wheel sensitivity; Settings > Control > Key map > Show values shows the control and its value over the picture as it changes. Hold the mouse button (or press the trackpad) on the picture and its center follows the mouse; let go and it stays there (Origin Motion becomes Park, and Edit > Undo puts it back). Entering or leaving full screen doesn't move the origin, and an off-center origin keeps its place in the picture at any size, so a patch looks the same in the window and full screen (Windows Whorld keeps the distance from the middle in pixels instead, so there an off-center origin moves toward the middle on a bigger picture). The renderer is set in Settings > Rendering: Metal (the GPU, the default) or Core Graphics (the CPU). Metal needs an Apple silicon Mac; on an Intel Mac, or if Metal fails, Whorld draws with Core Graphics. There too, Frame rate can hold the picture to 60 fps: rings move a step each frame, so on a 120 Hz display patches otherwise run twice as fast as on a 60 Hz monitor, and 60 fps uses less power. Exports (images, image sequences and videos) are drawn with Metal whenever this Mac can, whatever the Renderer setting says, so they come out the same every time; Intel Macs export with Core Graphics, which is slower with antialiasing on. Render scale (Settings > Rendering, or View > Render Scale) trades sharpness for speed on heavy patches or a slower Mac: at 75%, 50% or 33% the picture is drawn with fewer pixels and scaled up to fill the window or screen. Rings and lines keep their size; only edges get coarser. At 50% the graphics work is roughly a third to a half of full resolution's, so a patch that drops frames may run smoothly again (work that doesn't depend on pixels, such as building very complex rings, doesn't shrink). Upscaling chooses how it's enlarged: Smooth, or Sharp pixels, which keeps the pixels square and crisp (most even at 50%) and suits patches drawn without antialiasing. Exports aren't affected; Syphon gets the smaller frames unless its size is fixed.

Syphon

Syphon output sends Whorld's picture to other apps on the same Mac, such as Resolume, MadMapper, OBS and TouchDesigner, where it's listed as Whorld. Turn it on with View > Syphon Output or in Whorld > Settings > Syphon (it's off until you do). Transparency comes through: where a patch's Bk Alpha or Alpha is below 1, the receiving app can layer Whorld over other material. By default Syphon carries the frames Whorld shows, at their size (the window's, or the display's in full screen, times the Render scale). Settings > Syphon > Size > Fixed sends a size of your choosing instead, such as 1920 × 1080, whatever the window's shape: each frame is drawn a second time at that size, with the picture scaled to fit and more of the pattern on the longer side. The fixed-size frame is drawn only while an app is receiving it. Settings shows whether an app is receiving.

OSC and TouchOSC

Everything MIDI can map can also be controlled by OSC, over the network. Whorld listens on UDP port 8000 and answers on port 8001 (Whorld > Settings > Control, which also shows this Mac's address). It sends its state to every device that has sent it something in the last 30 seconds, and to "Also send to" if set: the values of the controls below as they change, and once a second the frame statistics. macOS may ask whether Whorld can use the local network: allow it, or other devices can't reach it.

TouchOSC: the DMG's TouchOSC folder has Whorld.tosc, a layout for a tablet or a computer with four pages: MAIN (Master, the Image switches and actions, the origin), PARAMS, GLOBALS and MONITOR. In TouchOSC set a connection to UDP, host = the Whorld Mac's address (127.0.0.1 on the same Mac), send port 8000, receive port 8001; open the layout and start it (▶). It shows "connected" when Whorld answers.

Addresses start with /w. Numbers are what a MIDI mapping sends: 0 to 1 covers a slider's range (values beyond act like a mapping with a wider Start and End). Switches take 1 or 0 (no argument toggles); actions happen on a message with no argument or a nonzero one.

MIDI

Whorld listens to MIDI input through Core MIDI. Choose the input device in Settings. To map a control, open MIDI Mapping, select one or more mappings, turn on MIDI Learn (⇧⌘L) and move the control on your device.

Besides the targets Windows Whorld has, the Mac adds Hue, Rotate Hue, Invert Color, and for audio modulation Mark Downbeat (on a press, not on its release), Modulation On and Modulation Depth (the Modulation window's switch and slider). Mappings are saved in the playlist; those to the Mac's targets go in a section of their own, which Windows Whorld doesn't read, so the playlist still opens there with the rest of its mappings (saving it on Windows leaves the Mac's out).

Audio modulation

Whorld can move with music. Choose Window > Modulation… and pick an Input: an audio interface, the built-in microphone, or a loopback device such as BlackHole that carries another app's sound (the first time, macOS asks whether Whorld may use audio input; the sound is analyzed as it arrives and never recorded or sent anywhere). On macOS 14.2 and later, Sound from apps in the same menu listens straight to an app (VLC, Music, Ableton Live, a browser) or to Everything the Mac plays, with no loopback device, and the sound still plays as usual. Apps appear once they've used sound ("quiet" when they aren't playing now); if the app quits, Whorld picks it up again when it plays. The first time, macOS asks whether Whorld may record system audio; if it's refused, Whorld hears only silence, and the status line says where to allow it (System Settings > Privacy & Security > Screen & System Audio Recording). Each new version of Whorld asks again. Channels picks which of the device's inputs to listen to, mixed together. Gain boosts or cuts what Whorld hears, Range sets how far below the top a sound can be and still register (36 dB suits mastered music, which lives in its top 15 to 20 dB; a smaller range makes the music move things more), Auto-level follows the music's recent peak so quiet and loud material both use the whole range, and Hit sensitivity sets how strong a beat must be to count as a hit. Hearing shows what the analysis hears: the overall level, four bands (Bass up to 150 Hz, Low Mid to 600 Hz, High Mid to 4 kHz, Treble above), Brightness (where the sound's energy sits, low to high), and lights for hits: any hit, a Kick (in the bass) or Hats (in the treble), each with how many it has counted a second over the last 4 seconds (on a track with a kick on every beat, Kick times 60 is the tempo). Below them, Beat: a beat tracker learns the music's tempo from the hits (it shows it once it has the beat, after a few seconds) and keeps a steady beat going, on the kick, even where a busy mix hides kicks from the hit detectors, and through a breakdown; it stops a few seconds after the music does. For music with a steady beat, Beat is usually a better source than Kick. Once it has the beat it also counts bars (four beats each) and phrases, shown as "bar 5 · beat 2" (bars 1 to 32), and these are sources too: Bar fires on the first beat of every bar, 4-Bar, 8-Bar, 16-Bar and 32-Bar on the first beat of every 4, 8, 16 or 32 bars, so a row can, say, play the next patch every 16 bars. Counting starts on the first beat after the tracker finds the beat, which may not be the music's "one": press Mark Downbeat on the one (a drop, the start of a phrase; also Image > Mark Downbeat, ⌥⌘8, or OSC /w/mod/downbeat) and the beat nearest your press becomes beat 1 of bar 1. A press a little late counts too, and fires the bar and phrase sources at once.

Ableton Link keeps Whorld in time with Ableton Live and other Link apps on this Mac or the local network. Turn it on with the Ableton Link switch (Enabled or Disabled). While the Link session has other apps in it, the beat comes from Link instead of from the music: Beat, Bar and the phrases follow the session's tempo, beats and bars exactly, with no time to lock on, and the Beat line shows "Ableton Link", the tempo and where you are. Bars line up with the session's (four beats each). Link keeps apps together within a bar but doesn't share where phrases start, so press Mark Downbeat on the one; with Start/Stop sync on, the beat where the session starts playing (Live's Play) is the one automatically, and the beat stops while the session is stopped. With no other Link apps, Whorld goes back to the beat it hears. If the connection drops (a Wi-Fi hiccup), Whorld keeps the beat going at the last tempo for up to 30 seconds and tries to reconnect every 10 seconds ("Reconnecting…"); turning Ableton Link off and on again does the same at once. Whorld's Tempo follows the beat makes Master > Tempo (which times Origin Random) follow the beat's tempo, from Link or from the music, while there is one, without changing the patch. Link uses Ableton's Link library (GPL version 2 or later).

Oscillators on the Beat (the second tab below the meters) locks a parameter's oscillator to the beat, from Link or from the music: one cycle every so many beats (a sixteenth note up to 32 bars), starting on the one (Mark Downbeat). Phase moves where the one falls within the cycle, in degrees (with a triangle, 0 puts the one halfway up, 90 at the top, 270 at the bottom), and Offset moves it a little earlier or later, in milliseconds. Only the timing changes: the patch's waveform, amplitude and pulse width are kept, so a parameter whose amplitude is 0 doesn't move (the last column says so). Without a beat, a locked oscillator carries on at the last tempo; turned off, or with Modulation off, it runs at the patch's own frequency again, from where it was. The patch itself never changes.

File > Export Audio-Reactive Video… makes a video of the current patch moved by a music file, with the music as its sound. Choose the file, then the frame size, frame rate, format, and how much of the file. The rows in the Modulation window (and its Depth, Tempo and audio settings) move the patch as they would live, but frame by frame, so every hit lands on its frame and the same settings make the same video every time. The video starts with the patch's first frame and reacts to the music from the start; turn on Run the patch first to let the patch fill the picture for some seconds before the video begins. The beat comes from the music, or from a tempo you set. From the music, Whorld looks ahead in the file: the beat-driven rows (Beat, Bar … 32-Bar, and the oscillators on the beat) start on the music's first beat, so a silent or ambient intro stays free of them, and that first beat is "the one" for the bars and phrases. With a set tempo they start at the downbeat, if you mark one. Mark the downbeat gives the time (in seconds into the file) of a "one" instead, for Bar … 32-Bar and the oscillators on the beat. Next and Previous Patch step through the playlist; snapshot actions are skipped. The video is framed as the view shows the patch, and you can keep playing while it renders; the progress appears at the bottom of the view, with Cancel.

The table below says what the sound moves. Each row has a Source (a level, a band, Brightness, a kind of hit, the beat, or the Beat LFO), a Target (any parameter's value or Global column, any Master control, Hue, or the origin), and an Amount: how far the target moves, as a share of its slider, when the source is at its loudest (negative moves it down). The target starts from the patch's own value, and the patch itself never changes: saving keeps its values, it isn't marked Edited, and turning modulation off brings it back exactly. Rows on the same target add up. Curve shapes the response: Linear; Soft (quiet passages count for more); Hard (only loud passages count); Gate (all or nothing above the Threshold). Polarity Up moves the target from its value upward; Centered moves it either side. Attack and Release (in milliseconds) smooth the movement as the sound rises and falls; a hit sends a pulse that fades over its Release. Offset moves a hit or beat row's timing, from −300 to +300 milliseconds: Beat and the bars fire that much before (negative) or after the beat, since the beat tracker knows where the next beat will fall, so a zoom pulse can lead the kick while a patch change lands just after the drop. A hit (Hit, Kick, Hats) can only be held back, as it can't be foreseen, so a negative offset does nothing for it. The Beat LFO is a wave locked to the beat (from Link or from the music) instead of a sound: the menu beside it sets its Length (a sixteenth note up to 32 bars), its Shape (Sine, Triangle, Ramp Up, Ramp Down or Square, as the parameters' waveforms) and its Phase. A cycle starts on the one (Mark Downbeat); Sine and Triangle start halfway up, rising, Ramp Up at the bottom, Ramp Down and Square at the top. It isn't smoothed, so Attack and Release don't apply, and its Offset moves it early or late. Without a beat it carries on at the last tempo; before the first beat it stays at 0. With Polarity Centered it swings either side of the patch's value, as an oscillator does. Live shows each row's level as it plays. A row whose Target is an action (Random Phase, Random Origin, Rotate Hue, Clear, Next or Previous Patch, Next or Previous Snapshot) fires it on each hit, or when a sound rises past the Threshold; Rotate Hue changes the patch's Hue, as it does from the menu.

Depth scales every row at once, and Modulation on (Image > Audio Modulation, ⌘8) switches them all off and on; both also work by OSC (/w/mod/on, /w/mod/depth). The rows are a setting, the same for every patch and playlist; Export and Import keep them in a file, and Restore Defaults brings back the starting rows (bass widens the lines, a kick pulses the zoom). Modulation needs a little time to respond: about 20 to 40 milliseconds from a sound to the picture.

Keeping it smooth. Listening and analyzing cost almost nothing, but some targets make Whorld draw much more: Ring Spacing moved down, or Rings, Line Width, Copies or Canvas moved up, add rings or make them heavier, just as moving those sliders by hand does. On a heavy patch, large amounts on them can drop the frame rate when the music is loud (View > Show Frame Rate shows it). If the picture stutters on loud passages, lower those rows' Amount or the Depth, or move something cheaper: Zoom, Hue, Speed, Star Factor, Pinwheel, the curves and shears, and the origin cost nothing extra.

Movies and video

Movies record every frame's geometry, so they can be replayed and exported at any size. Besides exporting an image sequence, the Mac version can export a movie straight to a QuickTime video (Movie > Export Video…) as H.264, HEVC, ProRes 422 or ProRes 4444. ProRes 4444 keeps the colors nearly exactly; the other formats soften color at sharp edges.

Hue, Rotate Hue and Invert Color

These three color controls from Whorld 1 work again in Whorld for Mac (Windows Whorld V2 keeps the settings in patches but doesn't use them). Hue (Master pane, 0 to 360 degrees) shifts the color of new rings; existing rings keep theirs, so a change sweeps outward as they're replaced. With Loop Hue on, it shifts the range of colors being looped through. Image > Rotate Hue (F3) turns the colors of the rings already showing by 90 degrees and adds 90 to Hue, so new rings follow; four in a row come back to where you started. Undo puts Hue back, but not the colors already turned. Image > Invert Color (F4, or Properties) inverts the colors of all rings, new and existing, as a photographic negative: hue and lightness both flip, so while it's on, raising Lightness darkens the rings. The background isn't affected. All three are saved with the patch and can be MIDI mapping targets or set by OSC. Normally a patch's colors carry on from where the previous patch left them, as on Windows, so the same patch can look different each time; to have each patch start from its own Hue, choose Whorld > Settings > Rendering > Patch colors > Start from the patch.

Fading rings

Normally, once there are as many rings as Master > Rings allows, the oldest ring vanishes each time a new one appears, and rings that grow past the edge of the picture, or past the canvas (Master > Canvas, around the rings' center), first simply leave it or vanish. Master > Fade makes them fade out instead: at 20, for example, each ring fades smoothly from its own transparency to nothing over its last 20 rings before it would vanish or leave the picture, whichever comes first. That holds however small Canvas is: rings fade out before the canvas removes them. A ring counts as gone once all of it is outside the picture, so with the rings' center off to one side they fade out toward the farthest corner. Fade goes from 0 (off, as in Windows Whorld) to 200, is saved with the patch, and can be a MIDI mapping target. Whorld > Settings > Rendering > Ring fade curve chooses between Linear, an even fade, and Eased, which starts and ends gently. The fade applies to the picture, snapshots, movies and exports; snapshots and movies don't record the Rings setting, so their own ring count stands in for it. With Rings at its maximum (unlimited), rings fade only at the edge of the picture. Rings that shrink toward the center (negative Ring Growth) fade only before the Rings limit. Patches with Fade above 0 still open in Windows Whorld, which ignores Fade.

Updates

Once a day Whorld asks GitHub whether a newer version has been released (only Whorld's version number is sent). If there is one, it shows what's new, with a button that opens the download page; install it as before, by dragging Whorld from the DMG to Applications. If you're running a beta, you're offered new betas too. Nothing is announced while Whorld is full screen; you'll hear about it when you leave. Choose Whorld > Check for Updates to check now, or turn off automatic checks in Whorld > Settings > General.

Bugs and ideas

Help > Report a Bug and Help > Request a Feature open a form on GitHub, with your Whorld version, macOS version and Mac filled in. You need a free GitHub account to send it. Requests from others are listed on the feature requests page; a 👍 on one helps decide what comes next.

First launch

This build is not notarized by Apple, so the first time you open it macOS says it can't check the app for malicious software. Control-click Whorld in the Applications folder, choose Open, then Open again. On macOS 15 and later, if there is no Open button, go to System Settings > Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway. You only need to do this once.
