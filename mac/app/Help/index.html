Whorld for Mac

A native macOS port of Whorld, the free sacred-geometry visualizer by Chris Korda.

Whorld draws animated rings of polygons and stars. You change how they grow, spin, skew and color with the parameters, and play them live with the keyboard or a MIDI controller. The Mac version is based on Whorld V2 and draws a patch the same way the Windows version does.

The detailed documentation for each parameter and feature is the Whorld manual. It was written for Whorld V1 on Windows, so some of what it says about menus, dialogs and installation differs from this app; this page lists the differences.

Files

Patches (.whp), playlists (.whl), snapshots (.whs) and movies (.whm) are the same files Windows Whorld uses, and can be moved between the two in either direction. Double-click a patch or playlist in the Finder to open it, or choose it from File > Open Recent. When you quit, Whorld remembers the playlist and patch you had open (the patch file, or the playlist entry that was playing) and opens them again next time; an untitled patch or a playlist that was never saved starts fresh, and a file that has since been moved is still found. To start with an untitled patch instead, as Windows Whorld does, turn off Whorld > Settings > General > Reopen the last playlist and patch at launch. Playing a patch from the playlist (or by MIDI or OSC) replaces the one showing without asking to save it, so you can move between patches freely while performing; to keep changes, save them first (⌘S). File > New, File > Open and Quit still ask. A patch can name its author: the window's title bar shows the playing patch's name and author, and the Playlist has an Author column. Set the author in the Save As dialog's Author field, or with File > Patch Author… (then save). For a patch that has no author yet, both suggest Whorld > Settings > General > Your name. The author is a line of its own in the patch file, which Windows Whorld ignores (and leaves out if it saves the patch again).

Windows and panels

As on Windows, the Whorld window holds the picture in the middle and the panels around it: Parameters, Master, Globals, Properties, Playlist, Modulation, MIDI Mapping and Key Map. Click the ⓘ by a panel's title for what it controls. Show or hide each from the View menu, or with ⌥⌘ and its number there: ⌥⌘1 Parameters, ⌥⌘2 Master, ⌥⌘3 Globals, ⌥⌘4 Properties, ⌥⌘5 Playlist, ⌥⌘6 Modulation, ⌥⌘7 MIDI Mapping, ⌥⌘8 Key Map. The panels sit in a left, a right and a bottom area; drag the dividers between them to resize. To move a panel, drag its title bar onto another panel: near one of that panel's edges, it goes above, below, left or right of it; on its title bar or in its middle, the two are stacked as tabs, as on Windows (what will happen is highlighted while you drag). Click a tab to show its panel, and drag a tab to move that panel out of the stack. A panel's View menu shortcut brings it to the front when it's a tab behind another. With the pointer over a panel, ⌥⌘ and an arrow moves it a step that way: beside the next panel, or into the next area (up from the bottom row into the side column on that half of the window, down from a side column into the bottom row, across between the side columns); with ⇧⌥⌘ it goes all the way, along that whole side of its area (full width above or below the bottom row, say). Over a floating panel, ⌥⌘←, ⌥⌘→ and ⌥⌘↓ dock it on the left, right or bottom. The title bar flashes, also when the panel can go no further. The panel's ⋯ menu lists these moves too. To have a panel span a whole area (across all the panels along the bottom, say, which suits the wide Modulation panel), drop it in the thin strip along that area’s outer side: the bottom of the window, just under a bottom panel’s title bar for the area’s top, or the strip along the picture’s edge next to that area; the highlight then runs the area’s whole length. Or drop it near the left, right or bottom edge of the picture to add it to that area, or use the ⋯ menu on its title bar, which can also float it in a window of its own (its Dock button puts it back). Drag a panel's title bar anywhere that isn't a dock spot (out of the Whorld window, or onto the middle of the picture, say) and let go, and it floats in a window there (let go over its own pane and it stays put); drag a floating panel's window by its title bar over the Whorld window, and the pane or edge it would dock at is highlighted (the window turns see-through to show it): let go there to dock it. Floating panels stay above the Whorld windows while Whorld is the active app (behind another app's windows when it isn't), and a panel hidden while floating floats again when shown. Modulation is a panel too: it opens floating, and docked in a narrower pane it puts its settings above Hearing (and scrolls, in a short one). Window > Show All Panels shows every panel and fits the Whorld window to its screen; Window > Reset Layout puts everything back in its standard place: Parameters on the left beside the picture, and Playlist, Properties, Master and Globals along the bottom. The arrangement is remembered between launches. To keep an arrangement for later, choose Window > Save Layout…, which saves the panels, their sizes and the windows in a .whlayout file; Window > Open Layout… (or double-clicking the file) brings it back, adapted to the screen. A playlist can carry a layout too: tick Include the panel layout in the Save Playlist As dialog, and opening the playlist arranges the panels that way (turn this off in Whorld > Settings > General > Open playlists with their layout). Windows Whorld ignores a playlist's layout. In the panels, click a slider and use ← → to move it in fine steps, ⇧← ⇧→ for larger steps, and ⇧⌘← ⇧⌘→ to jump to either end; in a number box, ↑ ↓ do the same (fine, with ⇧ larger, with ⇧⌘ to the end of the range, where the range has one). Hover over a control's name in Parameters, Master, Globals or Properties for a short explanation of what it does (Whorld > Settings > General > Show tips on control names turns these off). Right-click (or Control-click) a slider or number box to set it back to its value in the patch as it was loaded (opened, played from the playlist, or new) or last saved. On a trackpad, rest the pointer on a slider and swipe sideways with two fingers to move it, or on a number box and swipe up or down to step it (with ⇧ held, in larger steps). A mouse wheel works too: over a slider each click moves it 1% of its range, and over a number box each click is one step (larger with ⇧). A swipe or turn of the wheel that starts anywhere else scrolls the panel as usual, even as controls pass under the pointer; pause for half a second before turning the wheel over a control that scrolling brought under the pointer. In Properties, click a row, then ↑ ↓ move through the list and Space toggles the row (elsewhere, Space pauses and ⇧Space steps, even with a number box or the playlist selected; in a text field, such as a name, Space types a space). View > Show Stats (⌥⌘9) adds a thin strip along the top of the picture, never over it (in the picture’s own window when it’s detached): frames a second, refreshes missed in the last second (orange when any are), the time to draw a frame (CPU/GPU, in ms) and the rings drawn; the last control you changed (in a panel or a menu, by MIDI or OSC, or by swiping over the picture; not by modulation), with its value; and Quality (Render quality) and Auto (Automatic quality), the quick levers if frames drop, with what Automatic quality has lowered beside Auto. The options dialog is Whorld > Settings (⌘,).

View > Detach (⇧⌘D) moves the picture into a window of its own, as on Windows, and the panels then fill the Whorld window. Detach again, or close the picture's window, to put it back.

Full screen and projectors

View > Enter Full Screen (⇧⌘F) fills the display the Whorld window is on; ⇧⌘F or Esc returns to the window. F11, the Windows shortcut, works too where the keyboard sends it to apps; on most Mac keyboards F11 is a media key, and fn-F11 shows the desktop. To show Whorld on a projector or second display, choose View > Detach, drag the picture's window onto that display, then press ⇧⌘F. On a second display the menu bar and Dock stay on your main display, so the panels remain usable there while the picture fills the projector. While the picture is detached, its place in the Whorld window stays as a blank swipe pad that works like the picture, so you needn’t move the pointer to the other display: scroll or swipe there to zoom (with ⇧, Damping), hold a Key Map key to change its control, two-finger tap with the key held to put it back, press ⌘← ⌘→ for snapshots, and press and drag to move the origin (the pad stands for the whole picture). The stats strip (View > Show Stats) is on the pad then too. Drag the dividers to make the pad smaller. On the main display, full screen hides the menu bar and Dock (they don't slide in; press ⇧⌘F or Esc to leave). If the second display is disconnected, Whorld leaves full screen. In full screen the pointer is hidden while it's over the picture. Image > Next Patch (⌘↓) plays the playlist's next patch and Previous Patch (⌘↑) the previous one (after the last comes the first again; with none playing, they start at the first or the last), wherever the pointer is, a number box included. In full screen, or with the pointer over the picture, with snapshots loaded, ⌘→ and ⌘← show the next and previous snapshot. Over the picture, in the window or full screen, scrolling (a two-finger swipe up or down, or the mouse wheel) zooms, and with ⇧ changes Damping. Hold a key while you scroll to change something else instead (up increases); with the key still held, a two-finger tap (or a right click) puts that control back the same way, to its value when the patch was loaded or last saved. View > Key Map (⌥⌘8), a panel like the others (it opens floating; dock it beside the picture to keep it in view while you play), lists which key moves which control, and lets you change them. To change a key, click it and press the new one; a key can move one control, and assigning a key that's in use takes it from the other row. Any parameter column (Value, Amplitude, Frequency, Pulse Width, Global), Master row, Hue or the origin can have a key, each with its own speed. The starting map puts ring shape on the top letter row (Q Ring Spacing, W Line Width, E Ring Growth, R Rings, T Ring Sides, Y Star Factor, U Pinwheel, I Rotate Speed, O Aspect Ratio, P Skew Radius, [ Skew Angle), color on the home row (A Color Speed, S Speed, D Damping, F Fade, G Saturation, H Hue, J Lightness, K Alpha, L Hue Span), Master and the background on the bottom row (Z Zoom, X Trail, C Canvas, V Copies, B Tempo, N Spread, M Bk Hue, comma Bk Lightness, period Bk Saturation, slash Bk Alpha), and curves and the origin on the digits (1 Even Curve, 2 Odd Curve, 3 Even Shear, 4 Odd Shear, 9 Origin X, 0 Origin Y); File > Key Map > Restore Defaults brings it back, and Export… and Import… there keep a map in a file. The map is a setting, the same for every patch and playlist. How far a mouse wheel's click goes is Settings > Control > Wheel sensitivity, and how far a two-finger swipe goes, here and on the panels' sliders and number boxes, is Trackpad sensitivity (×0.25 to ×4; at ×1, the usual, a slider's knob moves half as far as your fingers, for fine adjustments); View > Show Stats (⌥⌘9) shows the last control changed this way, and its value, in the strip above the picture. Hold the mouse button (or press the trackpad) on the picture and its center follows the mouse; let go and it stays there (Origin, in the Properties panel, becomes Park, and Edit > Undo puts it back). Entering or leaving full screen doesn't move the origin, and an off-center origin keeps its place in the picture at any size, so a patch looks the same in the window and full screen (Windows Whorld keeps the distance from the middle in pixels instead, so there an off-center origin moves toward the middle on a bigger picture). The renderer is set in Settings > Display: Metal (the GPU, the default) or Core Graphics (the CPU). Metal needs an Apple silicon Mac; on an Intel Mac, or if Metal fails, Whorld draws with Core Graphics. There too, Frame rate can hold the picture to 60 fps: rings move a step each frame, so on a 120 Hz display patches otherwise run twice as fast as on a 60 Hz monitor, and 60 fps uses less power. Exports (images, image sequences and videos) are drawn with Metal whenever this Mac can, whatever the Renderer setting says, so they come out the same every time; Intel Macs export with Core Graphics, which is slower with antialiasing on. Render quality (Quality in the stats strip, or Settings > Display) trades sharpness for speed on heavy patches or a slower Mac: at 75%, 50% or 33% the picture is drawn with fewer pixels and scaled up to fill the window or screen. Rings and lines keep their size; only edges get coarser. At 50% the graphics work is roughly a third to a half of full resolution's, so a patch that drops frames may run smoothly again (work that doesn't depend on pixels, such as building very complex rings, doesn't shrink). Upscaling chooses how it's enlarged: Smooth, or Sharp pixels, which keeps the pixels square and crisp (most even at 50%) and suits patches drawn without antialiasing. Exports aren't affected; Syphon gets the smaller frames unless its size is fixed. Curve quality (Settings > Display, a slider from Low to High) helps where Render quality can't, on patches with large curved rings such as bloom, typhoon and boomer: curves are drawn as many short straight segments, and lower quality uses fewer, so there's less to build and draw (boomer at full screen with antialiasing: about 54 fps at High, 69 at Low on an M1 Max). At Low, curves may look slightly faceted, by up to a pixel; straight-sided patches look the same at any setting. High is the default and how exports are always drawn. Automatic quality (Auto in the stats strip, or Settings > Display) does this for you: when the picture can't keep up with the display, Whorld lowers Curve quality first, then Render quality to 75% and 50%, a step at a time, and raises them again once there's room to spare, never above your settings. View > Show Stats shows where it stands, beside Auto (for example, "curves 1.0 px, 75%"). On an M1 Max at 120 Hz full screen, boomer settles at 75% scale and runs at a steady 120 fps; a change of scale costs one frame.

Syphon

Syphon output sends Whorld's picture to other apps on the same Mac, such as Resolume, MadMapper, OBS and TouchDesigner, where it's listed as Whorld. Turn it on with View > Syphon > Syphon Output, or the Syphon checkbox in the stats strip (View > Show Stats), where a dot shows whether an app is receiving: hollow while none is, filled once one is (it's off until you turn it on). Transparency comes through: where a patch's Bk Alpha or Alpha is below 1, the receiving app can layer Whorld over other material. By default Syphon carries the frames Whorld shows, at their size (the window's, or the display's in full screen, times the Render quality). View > Syphon also sends a size of your choosing instead: 1280 × 720, 1920 × 1080, 3840 × 2160, or Custom Size…; Match the Output goes back. A fixed size is sent whatever the window's shape: each frame is drawn a second time at that size, with the picture scaled to fit and more of the pattern on the longer side. The fixed-size frame is drawn only while an app is receiving it.

OSC and TouchOSC

Everything MIDI can map can also be controlled by OSC, over the network. Whorld listens on UDP port 8000 and answers on port 8001 (Whorld > Settings > Control, which also shows this Mac's address). It sends its state to every device that has sent it something in the last 30 seconds, and to "Also send to" if set: the values of the controls below as they change, and once a second the frame statistics. macOS may ask whether Whorld can use the local network: allow it, or other devices can't reach it.

TouchOSC: the DMG's TouchOSC folder has Whorld.tosc, a layout for a tablet or a computer with four pages: MAIN (Master, the Image switches and actions, the origin), PARAMS, GLOBALS and MONITOR. In TouchOSC set a connection to UDP, host = the Whorld Mac's address (127.0.0.1 on the same Mac), send port 8000, receive port 8001; open the layout and start it (▶). It shows "connected" when Whorld answers.

Addresses start with /w. Numbers are what a MIDI mapping sends: 0 to 1 covers a slider's range (values beyond act like a mapping with a wider Start and End). Switches take 1 or 0 (no argument toggles); actions happen on a message with no argument or a nonzero one.

MIDI

Whorld listens to MIDI input through Core MIDI. Choose the input device in Settings. To map a control, open MIDI Mapping, select one or more mappings, turn on the panel's Learn switch and move the control on your device.

Besides the targets Windows Whorld has, the Mac adds Hue, Rotate Hue, Invert Color, and for audio modulation Reset to 1.1 (on a press, not on its release), Modulation On and Modulation Depth (the Modulation window's switch and slider), Beat Back, Beat Forward, Bar Back, Bar Forward and Half Beat (the Modulation window's beat buttons, on a press), Reset Bar Count (on a press: the nearest bar becomes bar 1, the beat kept), and Geometry Mode (the patch mode: on is Geometry). Mappings are saved in the playlist; those to the Mac's targets go in a section of their own, which Windows Whorld doesn't read, so the playlist still opens there with the rest of its mappings (saving it on Windows leaves the Mac's out).

Audio modulation

Whorld can move with music. The Audio menu gathers it: the rows on or off, the beat corrections, Ableton Link and Tempo Follows the Beat. Choose View > Modulation (⌥⌘6), a panel like the others (it opens floating; dock it as any panel), and pick an Input: an audio interface, the built-in microphone, or a loopback device such as BlackHole that carries another app's sound (the first time, macOS asks whether Whorld may use audio input; the sound is analyzed as it arrives and never recorded or sent anywhere). On macOS 14.2 and later, Sound from apps in the same menu listens straight to an app (VLC, Music, Ableton Live, a browser) or to Everything the Mac plays, with no loopback device, and the sound still plays as usual. Apps appear once they've used sound ("quiet" when they aren't playing now); if the app quits, Whorld picks it up again when it plays. The first time, macOS asks whether Whorld may record system audio; if it's refused, Whorld hears only silence, and the status line says where to allow it (System Settings > Privacy & Security > Screen & System Audio Recording). Each new version of Whorld asks again. Channels picks which of the device's inputs to listen to, mixed together. Gain boosts or cuts what Whorld hears, Range sets how far below the top a sound can be and still register (36 dB suits mastered music, which lives in its top 15 to 20 dB; a smaller range makes the music move things more), Auto-level follows the music's recent peak so quiet and loud material both use the whole range, and Hit sensitivity sets how strong a beat must be to count as a hit. Hearing shows what the analysis hears: the overall level, four bands (Bass up to 150 Hz, Low Mid to 600 Hz, High Mid to 4 kHz, Treble above), Brightness (where the sound's energy sits, low to high), and lights for hits: any hit, a Kick (in the bass) or Hats (in the treble), each with how many it has counted a second over the last 4 seconds (on a track with a kick on every beat, Kick times 60 is the tempo). Below them, Beat: a beat tracker learns the music's tempo from the hits (it shows it once it has the beat, after a few seconds) and keeps a steady beat going, on the kick, even where a busy mix hides kicks from the hit detectors, and through a breakdown; it stops a few seconds after the music does. For music with a steady beat, Beat is usually a better source than Kick. Once it has the beat it also counts bars (four beats each) and phrases, shown as "bar 5 · beat 2" (bars 1 to 32), and a row can pulse with them: choose Beats or Bars as its source, and a number box appears in front, for a pulse every so many beats or bars (any number: 1 bar, 16 bars, 3 beats for a polyrhythm, 1/3 beat for triplets), so a row can, say, play the next patch every 16 bars. The Phase menu beside it moves the pulse that far into each cycle (with 4 bars, 90° is bar 2, 180° bar 3). Older settings' Beat, Bar and 4-Bar … 32-Bar rows open as 1 beat, 1 bar, 4 bars … 32 bars. Counting starts on the first beat after the tracker finds the beat, which may not be the music's "one". To keep the rows' phrases with the music's, the buttons beside the meter: press Reset to 1.1 on the one (a drop, the start of a phrase; also Audio > Reset to 1.1, ⌘\, or OSC /w/mod/downbeat) and the beat nearest your press becomes beat 1 of bar 1, a little early or late alike. − and + move the count a beat back or on (with ⇧, a bar; Audio > Beat Back ⌘[, Beat Forward ⌘], Bar Back ⇧⌘[, Bar Forward ⇧⌘]), for a count that's off by a beat or a bar, without waiting for the next one in the music. Half Beat Back and Half Beat Forward (Audio menu, ⌥⌘[ and ⌥⌘], or the panel's − and + with ⌥) move the count half a beat, the beat itself half a beat later or earlier, twice making a whole beat, for when the tracker has found the off-beats (the Beat light flashes between the kicks); it stays shifted as the tracker follows the music. None of these fires a row on its own, and waves locked to the beat (the Beat LFO, Frequency rows) ease to their new place over a beat instead of jumping. Phrase, under the Beat light, shows the bar of an 8-bar phrase the count is in, orange on its one, so you can see whether the rows' phrases start where the music's do. Each also has a MIDI target and an OSC address. A press a little late counts too, and fires the bar and phrase sources at once.

Ableton Link keeps Whorld in time with Ableton Live and other Link apps on this Mac or the local network. Turn it on with the Ableton Link switch (Enabled or Disabled). While the Link session has other apps in it, the beat comes from Link instead of from the music: Beat, Bar and the phrases follow the session's tempo, beats and bars exactly, with no time to lock on, and the Beat line shows "Ableton Link", the tempo and where you are. Bars line up with the session's (four beats each). Link keeps apps together within a bar but doesn't share where phrases start, so press Reset to 1.1 on the one, or move the count by bars with − and + (by bars only: Link sets the beat within the bar, and ½ Beat is off); with Start/Stop sync on, the beat where the session starts playing (Live's Play) is the one automatically, and the beat stops while the session is stopped. With no other Link apps, Whorld goes back to the beat it hears. If the connection drops (a Wi-Fi hiccup), Whorld keeps the beat going at the last tempo for up to 30 seconds and tries to reconnect every 10 seconds ("Reconnecting…"); turning Ableton Link off and on again does the same at once. Audio > Tempo Follows the Beat makes Master > Tempo (which times Origin Random) follow the beat's tempo, from Link or from the music, while there is one, without changing the patch. Link uses Ableton's Link library (GPL version 2 or later).

Oscillators on the beat. A row whose Target is a parameter's Frequency (in the Target menu, the parameter's submenu, such as Star Factor > Frequency) locks that parameter's oscillator to the beat, from Link or from the music, instead of moving a control. Its Source is the cycle's length: a number, then Beats or Bars (a bar is 4 beats), starting on the one (Reset to 1.1). The number can be anything from a sixteenth of a beat to 128 bars: whole (3 beats over a 4-beat bar makes a polyrhythm, back in line with the bar every 12 beats), a fraction such as 1/3 (triplets, exactly) or a decimal. Click the box and type, or step it with ↑ ↓, a two-finger swipe up or down or the mouse wheel (by one, halving or doubling below one; with ⇧, halving or doubling); right-click it for 1. Changing Beats to Bars keeps the number; the menu beside it is the Phase, where the one falls within the cycle (with a triangle or sine, 0° puts the one halfway up, 90° at the top, 180° halfway down, 270° at the bottom). Offset moves it a little earlier or later, in milliseconds. If several rows lock the same oscillator, the last one that's on wins. Only the timing changes: the patch's waveform, amplitude and pulse width are kept, so a parameter whose amplitude is 0 doesn't move (the Amount column says so). Without a beat, a locked oscillator carries on at the last tempo; turned off, or with Modulation off, it runs at the patch's own frequency again, from where it was. The patch itself never changes.

File > Export > Audio-Reactive Video… makes a video of the current patch moved by a music file, with the music as its sound. Choose the file, then the frame size, frame rate, format, and how much of the file. The rows in the Modulation window (and its Depth, Tempo and audio settings) move the patch as they would live, but frame by frame, so every hit lands on its frame and the same settings make the same video every time. The video starts with the patch's first frame and reacts to the music from the start; turn on Run the patch first to let the patch fill the picture for some seconds before the video begins. The beat comes from the music, or from a tempo you set. From the music, Whorld looks ahead in the file: the beat-driven rows (Beat, Bar … 32-Bar, and the oscillators on the beat) start on the music's first beat, so a silent or ambient intro stays free of them, and that first beat is "the one" for the bars and phrases. With a set tempo they start at the downbeat, if you mark one. Mark the downbeat gives the time (in seconds into the file) of a "one" instead, for Bar … 32-Bar and the oscillators on the beat. Next and Previous Patch step through the playlist; snapshot actions are skipped. The video is framed as the view shows the patch, and you can keep playing while it renders; the progress appears at the bottom of the view, with Cancel.

The table below says what the sound moves. Each row has a Source (a level, a band, Brightness, a kind of hit, the beat, or the Beat LFO), a Target (any parameter's value, Global or Amplitude column, any Master control, Hue, or the origin; in the Target menu each parameter is a submenu of its columns, Value, Amplitude, Frequency and Global where it has one), and an Amount: how far the target moves, as a share of its slider, when the source is at its loudest (negative moves it down). An Amplitude target widens (or, negative, narrows) how far the parameter's oscillator swings, by the Amount as a share of the parameter's Value slider: Bass on Star Factor Amplitude makes the stars wobble harder on the bass. It only shows while that row's oscillator is running (Frequency above 0). The target starts from the patch's own value, and the patch itself never changes: saving keeps its values, it isn't marked Edited, and turning modulation off brings it back exactly. Rows on the same target add up. Curve shapes the response: Linear; Soft (quiet passages count for more); Hard (only loud passages count); Gate (all or nothing above the Threshold). Polarity Up moves the target from its value upward; Centered moves it either side. Attack and Release (in milliseconds) smooth the movement as the sound rises and falls; a hit sends a pulse that fades over its Release. The number boxes in a row (the length, Attack, Release, Offset and Threshold) work like the panels': type a value, or click one and step it with ↑ ↓ (larger steps with ⇧, to either end with ⇧⌘), a two-finger swipe up or down or the mouse wheel; right-click for its starting value. Attack and Release step by 10 ms (100 with ⇧), Offset by 1 ms (10), Threshold by 0.01 (0.1). Offset moves a hit or beat row's timing, from −300 to +300 milliseconds: Beat and the bars fire that much before (negative) or after the beat, since the beat tracker knows where the next beat will fall, so a zoom pulse can lead the kick while a patch change lands just after the drop. A hit (Hit, Kick, Hats) can only be held back, as it can't be foreseen, so a negative offset does nothing for it. The Beat LFO is a wave locked to the beat (from Link or from the music) instead of a sound: choose Beat LFO in the Source menu, and beside it set its length as for a Frequency row (a number, then Beats or Bars), then in the menu beside that its Shape (Sine, Triangle, Ramp Up, Ramp Down or Square, as the parameters' waveforms) and its Phase. A cycle starts on the one (Reset to 1.1); Sine and Triangle start halfway up, rising, Ramp Up at the bottom, Ramp Down and Square at the top. It isn't smoothed, so Attack and Release don't apply, and its Offset moves it early or late. Without a beat it carries on at the last tempo; before the first beat it stays at 0. With Polarity Centered it swings either side of the patch's value, as an oscillator does. Live shows each row's level as it plays. On the panels, while modulation is on, the slider of each control a row moves shows a second knob outlined in the accent color, where the control is at that moment; its normal knob keeps showing the patch's own value. In silence the outline rings the normal knob, so you can see at a glance which controls are modulated. A row whose Target is an action (Random Phase, Random Origin, Rotate Hue, Clear, Next or Previous Patch, Next or Previous Snapshot) fires it on each hit, or when a sound rises past the Threshold; Rotate Hue changes the patch's Hue, as it does from the menu.

At the top of the window, the Modulation switch turns every row off and on (also Audio > Audio Modulation, ⌘8), and Depth scales them all at once; both also work by OSC (/w/mod/on, /w/mod/depth). The rows are a setting, the same for every patch and playlist; Export and Import (top right) keep them in a file, and Restore Defaults brings back the starting rows (bass widens the lines, a kick pulses the zoom). The window's sliders (Depth, Gain, Range, Hit sensitivity and each row's Amount) work like the panels': swipe sideways with two fingers over one, or turn a mouse wheel, to move it; click it, then ← → step it (larger with ⇧); right-click it for its starting value (Depth 100%, Gain 0 dB, Range 36 dB, a row's Amount 25%). Modulation needs a little time to respond: about 20 to 40 milliseconds from a sound to the picture.

Keeping it smooth. Listening and analyzing cost almost nothing, but some targets make Whorld draw much more: Ring Spacing moved down, or Rings, Line Width, Copies or Canvas moved up, add rings or make them heavier, just as moving those sliders by hand does. On a heavy patch, large amounts on them can drop the frame rate when the music is loud (View > Show Stats shows it). If the picture stutters on loud passages, lower those rows' Amount or the Depth, or move something cheaper: Zoom, Hue, Speed, Star Factor, Pinwheel, the curves and shears, and the origin cost nothing extra.

Patch mode

Whorld 1's patch mode is back: Image > Geometry Only (⌥⌘G) switches between Full and Geometry mode, and so does the button in the bottom right corner of the Playlist panel, which is named after the mode it's in: Full or Geom. In Full mode, the usual one, playing or opening a patch changes everything, as on Windows. In Geometry mode it changes only the rings' shape and motion: Ring Growth, Ring Spacing, Line Width, Ring Sides, Rotate Speed, Aspect Ratio, Skew Radius, Skew Angle, Star Factor, Pinwheel and the curve and shear rows, each with its waveform, amplitude, frequency and pulse width. Everything else stays as it is: the ring and background colors (Color Speed, Lightness, Saturation, Alpha and their Bk rows), Hue, the Master controls, the Properties switches (Mirror, Fill, Convex, Antialiasing and the rest) and every Global value. That's for performing: change shapes from the playlist without losing the colors and effects you've set. A patch saved in Geometry mode is saved as you see it, with the kept settings.

The mode is saved with the playlist (Windows Whorld ignores it), and a MIDI or OSC control can switch it (the Geometry Mode target, /w/geometry). Switching it from the menu or the button marks the playlist edited; from MIDI or OSC it doesn't, so switching live doesn't leave a playlist to save.

Movies and video

Movies record every frame's geometry, so they can be replayed and exported at any size. Besides exporting an image sequence, the Mac version can export a movie straight to a QuickTime video (File > Export > Movie as Video…) as H.264, HEVC, ProRes 422 or ProRes 4444. ProRes 4444 keeps the colors nearly exactly; the other formats soften color at sharp edges.

Hue, Rotate Hue and Invert Color

These three color controls from Whorld 1 work again in Whorld for Mac (Windows Whorld V2 keeps the settings in patches but doesn't use them). Hue (Master pane, 0 to 360 degrees) shifts the color of new rings; existing rings keep theirs, so a change sweeps outward as they're replaced. With Loop Hue on, it shifts the range of colors being looped through. Image > Rotate Hue (F3) turns the colors of the rings already showing by 90 degrees and adds 90 to Hue, so new rings follow; four in a row come back to where you started. Undo puts Hue back, but not the colors already turned. Image > Invert Color (F4, or Properties) inverts the colors of all rings, new and existing, as a photographic negative: hue and lightness both flip, so while it's on, raising Lightness darkens the rings. The background isn't affected. All three are saved with the patch and can be MIDI mapping targets or set by OSC. Normally a patch's colors carry on from where the previous patch left them, as on Windows, so the same patch can look different each time; to have each patch start from its own Hue, choose Whorld > Settings > Display > Patch colors > Start from the patch.

Fading rings

Normally, once there are as many rings as Master > Rings allows, the oldest ring vanishes each time a new one appears, and rings that grow past the edge of the picture, or past the canvas (Master > Canvas, around the rings' center), first simply leave it or vanish. Master > Fade makes them fade out instead: at 20, for example, each ring fades smoothly from its own transparency to nothing over its last 20 rings before it would vanish or leave the picture, whichever comes first. That holds however small Canvas is: rings fade out before the canvas removes them. A ring counts as gone once all of it is outside the picture, so with the rings' center off to one side they fade out toward the farthest corner. Fade goes from 0 (off, as in Windows Whorld) to 200, is saved with the patch, and can be a MIDI mapping target. Whorld > Settings > Display > Ring fade curve chooses between Eased (the default), which starts and ends gently, and Linear, an even fade. The fade applies to the picture, snapshots, movies and exports; snapshots and movies don't record the Rings setting, so their own ring count stands in for it. With Rings at its maximum (unlimited), rings fade only at the edge of the picture. Rings that shrink toward the center (negative Ring Growth) fade only before the Rings limit. Patches with Fade above 0 still open in Windows Whorld, which ignores Fade.

Updates

Once a day Whorld asks GitHub whether a newer version has been released (only Whorld's version number is sent). If there is one, it shows what's new, with a button that opens the download page; install it as before, by dragging Whorld from the DMG to Applications. If you're running a beta, you're offered new betas too. Nothing is announced while Whorld is full screen; you'll hear about it when you leave. Choose Whorld > Check for Updates to check now, or turn off automatic checks in Whorld > Settings > General.

Bugs and ideas

Help > Report a Bug and Help > Request a Feature open a form on GitHub, with your Whorld version, macOS version and Mac filled in. You need a free GitHub account to send it. Requests from others are listed on the feature requests page; a 👍 on one helps decide what comes next.

First launch

This build is not notarized by Apple, so the first time you open it macOS says it can't check the app for malicious software. Control-click Whorld in the Applications folder, choose Open, then Open again. On macOS 15 and later, if there is no Open button, go to System Settings > Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway. You only need to do this once.
